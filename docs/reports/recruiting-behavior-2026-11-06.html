Price Engineering Assistant · Behavior change

How the assistant now answers future students

November 6, 2026 · Applies to new conversations once deployed · Research: recruiting research brief

When a future student or a parent asks something, the assistant now works out what they're really deciding and answers that with specific things the U offers, taken from pages it read. It stays honest: it doesn't compare the U with other schools, state numbers it didn't read, or push anyone to apply. Current students get the same help without the recruiting angle.

What changed

Before, the assistant answered the literal question the same way for everyone. Now, for future students and families, it answers the question behind the question. Most questions from prospective students are really about one of a few decisions, and the answer addresses that decision:

They askWhat they're usually weighingWhat the answer now includes
"What's [major] like?"Would I enjoy it, and where does it lead?What students build and do at the U (design projects, teams, research), where graduates work
"How much does it cost?"Can we afford it?The price with its year first, then scholarships and how to be considered
"Is the U good for [field]?"Is it a fit for me?Specific programs and labs the U's pages describe, and honest gaps
"Will I get a job?"Is it worth it?Co-ops and internships for credit, career fairs, and what the U does or doesn't publish about outcomes
"What should I major in?"What fits me, when I barely know what engineering isOne short question about the problems they'd like to work on, then two or three majors with a U example each
A deadline, a form, a buildingA taskJust the answer

Each specific comes with a few words on what it means for the student. It's added only when it helps answer their question, at the usual length, and with at most one next step that fits (an info session, a visit, an event where they can see students' work, or a question for a student ambassador). The widget's future-student suggestions now start with "Which engineering major is right for me?", which opens the major finder.

"What's mechanical engineering like at the U?"

…In your senior year you'd spend two semesters on a team of 4 to 6 building a project defined and funded by an industry sponsor, in a 3,500 sq ft lab with 3D printers, a laser cutter and a CNC plasma cutter. So you'd graduate with a real engineering project for a company on your résumé. Students also build for competitions like Formula SAE and Baja SAE. If you want to see the work, seniors show their projects at Design Day on December 8.

An illustration written from facts on mech.utah.edu, not a recorded answer. Real answers vary.

The behavior is written as one section of the assistant's instructions, "Future students and families". It describes behavior only, with no page names or figures, so the assistant still finds the specifics by searching the college's sites. It switches on when the visitor picks "Future student" or their messages show they or their child are choosing a college.

Why: what the research says

A research pass in November 2026 (six researchers, with checkers rereading the original sources) looked at what drives college choice and what recruiting chatbots have actually achieved.

48% vs 8%will choose the best overall fit, against 8% who choose on reputationPrinceton Review 2026, 9,446 respondents
39%of parents say internships, co-ops and active learning make college worth it; 38% say job placementEAB 2026 parent survey, 2,515 parents
35%name debt as their biggest worry about collegePrinceton Review 2026
+3.3 ptson-time enrollment from Georgia State's chatbot, among admits who had already committed. No effect on the undecided.Page & Gehlbach, randomized trial
FindingSo the assistant…
Students choose on fit to their own goals, not on being told a school is the best.Ties every specific to what the student said they want, instead of claiming the U is better.
Parents judge value by hands-on learning, job placement and earnings. Students most want majors, scholarships and cost information.Reaches first for hands-on work, research, co-ops, scholarships and where graduates work.
Chatbots help students take concrete steps; they don't persuade. Generic nudges sent to more than 800,000 students had no effect.Offers one next step that fits the question, never sales copy.
Hidden promotion in chatbot answers often goes unnoticed, but once people notice it they rate the bot manipulative and less trustworthy. Students trust AI because they think it isn't selling.Adds only what serves the question, so there's nothing hidden to notice.
Pressuring, freedom-limiting wording makes people resist. Suggestions that leave the choice to them are accepted.Leaves the decision to them and suggests talking it over with family, a counselor or an adviser.
Most teens know little about engineering, and framing majors by the problems they solve raises interest.Asks one question about the problems they'd like to work on before suggesting majors.
LLMs give stereotyped major advice when they infer things about a person.Asks about interests only, never gender or background.

The survey figures come from companies that sell enrollment services (EAB, Princeton Review, RNL), so treat them as directional. The chatbot studies were checked against the original papers.

What it deliberately won't do

It won'tWhy
Compare the U with other schools, or comment on themThe admissions profession's ethics guide (NACAC, 2026) says not to spread "inaccurate, misleading or disparaging information" about other colleges. The FTC's $100M settlement with DeVry centered on a claim that its graduates out-earned other colleges'. Federal data on U vs. Utah State earnings is mixed anyway.
State salaries, placement rates or rankings it didn't read on a U pageFederal misrepresentation rules (34 CFR 668.71–75) cover what a university and its representatives say about graduates' jobs and pay. In January 2025 the Education Department fined Baker College $2.5M, partly for using national salary data instead of its own graduates'.
Promise a job or a salaryThe same rules. It describes what the U offers instead.
Push: "you should apply", "spots are filling up"Federal rules on aggressive recruiting (34 CFR 668.501) bar pressure, false urgency and discouraging people from asking others.
Pretend to be a personIt says plainly that it's an AI assistant, which also meets Utah's AI disclosure law (Utah Code 13-75).
Recruit current studentsThey're already here; selling to them would undercut trust in the assistant.

How it's checked

New eval cases

  • 17 cases in a new recruiting category, 3 of them must-pass (a cost-and-value question isn't declined, a Utah State question isn't declined, and it says it's an AI).
  • New checks: the answer stays short, answers first, asks a question back, and uses only dollar figures and percentages it actually read.
  • Run them alone for about 3 cents: python -m evals.run answers --only recruiting.

Watching for side effects

  • The eval report now shows median answer length and model calls per answer. Before this change: 124 words and 1.67 calls (October 29 run).
  • If either rises noticeably, the behavior is padding answers or searching for selling points.
  • Results against the real bot are pending; that run costs about $0.18 and needs approval first.

Still open

  • Salaries by major. Only mechanical engineering publishes a U figure, and it's undated. Showing what graduates earn needs U Career Success to publish its first-destination results by major.
  • Stale college pages. Old "Pac-12" claims, an undated biomedical ranking and a 2013 salary post could surface more often now. They're listed in the research brief for the site audit.
  • The take-home summary (a visual recap a student can keep) needs logo approval from the college's communications office and a staff-approved fact list.
  • Questions for staff: who signs off on recruiting language, which scholarship count is official (290, 480 or 700 appear on different pages), and whether labeled federal data such as College Scorecard may be used.
